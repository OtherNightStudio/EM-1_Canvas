# Recording and sharing EM-1 drum and synth sounds

**We believe anyone who lawfully owns a drum machine should be free to record its factory sounds and redistribute those recordings as they see fit, including as individual drum hits.** An EM-1 owner can trigger each pad and record its output into a sampler. Canvas gets to the same factory hit by decoding the stored PCM. The output stage can color a line recording, but either route lets another musician load and play the hit. In our view, the choice of cable or decoder should make no difference to the owner’s freedom to share it.

## The sounds behind the sounds

We hear Roland TR-808 and TR-909 sounds in the factory bank. Korg’s wave list calls slot 10 **BD-88 1** and slot 2 **BD-99 1**. We also hear chops of the Amen break, the drum solo Gregory Coleman played on the Winstons’ 1969 recording of *Amen, Brother*. Korg lists **BD-Jungle**, **BD-Jungle/Crash**, and **SD-Jungle** in slots 21, 22, and 41. Those are documented preset names. The Roland and Amen attributions are identifications by ear. The wave list gives no recording credits or licensing history for those hits. [Korg EM-1 owner’s manual, Wave Name List](https://cdn.korg.com/us/support/download/files/8096be030e0971487633f14c3823a107.pdf?response-content-disposition=inline%3Bfilename%2A%3DUTF-8%27%27EM1_OM_E.pdf&response-content-type=application%2Fpdf%3B#page=56) · [Library of Congress on *Amen, Brother*](https://www.loc.gov/programs/national-recording-preservation-board/recording-registry/registry-by-induction-years/2026/)

This is where the gray area gets interesting. Record a kick from an original analog TR-808 and you capture a sound its circuit generates on that trigger. Record the EM-1 playing an 808-style kick and you capture a waveform someone previously fixed in the bank. If a jungle hit contains a chop from *Amen, Brother*, that same pad press also reproduces part of an earlier recorded performance. Any license to put that recording in an instrument would have its own terms, including who could redistribute the audio afterward. [Roland on the TR-808’s analog voices](https://www.roland.com/ca/promos/roland_tr-808/) · [17 U.S.C. § 114(b)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/114)

Under U.S. law, owning the instrument and owning copyrights in recordings inside it are separate matters. A very short, simple hit may lack enough original authorship for protection. Where a recording is protected, capturing it through the outputs can reproduce it just as decoding its PCM can. Public redistribution then depends on the applicable permissions or legal exceptions, including fair use. That assessment depends on the particular recording and use. [17 U.S.C. § 202](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/202) · [U.S. Copyright Office on short recordings](https://www.copyright.gov/comp3/chap800/ch800-performing-arts.pdf#page=39) · [Fair use](https://www.copyright.gov/fair-use/)

## Why we chose Roland wave ROMs for the synth parts

The EM-1’s two synth parts share 50 factory waves. A single wave can use several recordings across the keyboard. Play a low note on **Saw-ProSynth** and the EM-1 picks one of ten zones, uses its root pitch to tune it, and repeats a short loop to sustain it. That design lets a few hundred samples carry a note for seconds in a machine with very little sample memory. [A worked example from the factory bank](./EM1_Firmware_and_PCM.md)

We chose Roland’s **SR-JV80 expansion boards** and the **JD-990’s internal wave ROMs** because they already organize sounds the way the EM-1 needs them. The ROM records which keys each sample covers, where it loops, and how it is tuned. Reading that information lets us move a playable multisample into a synth slot without recording every note or finding the loop points again by ear. Canvas decodes the samples and translates the map for the EM-1, whose own synth controls then shape the sound. A hardware test with a JD-990 sine wave confirmed that it tracked pitch across octaves. [Roland wave and sample tables](https://github.com/PythonBlue/SRJV/blob/main/FORMAT.md) · [EM-1 playback verification](./EM1_Firmware_and_PCM.md#what-was-verified)

Our starting point for the waveform interpreter was **PythonBlue’s SRJV**. Its format notes and extractor showed how to connect a named wave to its sample data. SRJV credits **hackyourlife** for working out how to undo the ROM scrambling and for the differential PCM encoding tools, and **jv80tinkerer** for the earlier wave extractor. We also used **giulioz’s VirtualJV** as a reference for PCM addressing and for reconstructing samples from their stored differences. Canvas implements its own reader and converts the result to the EM-1’s playback format. [SRJV and upstream credits](https://github.com/PythonBlue/SRJV#footnotes) · [srscramble](https://github.com/hackyourlife/srscramble) · [VirtualJV](https://github.com/giulioz/jv880_juce)

## What Canvas distributes

The release HTML includes Korg’s 144 factory drum hits, its 50 synth waves, and the complete factory PCM bank used by **Restore PCM**. Roland wave ROMs are optional files supplied by the user. **Save Bank** can embed a complete imported Roland ROM in the saved Canvas bank. Sharing that bank can therefore share the source ROM as well as the waves assigned to synth slots. [Canvas bank files](./README.md#bank)

The project’s MIT license covers its original code and documentation, including the waveform reader. Tracelistener’s contributions retain their MIT notice. The factory Korg audio and firmware, Roland ROM images, and waveforms decoded from them remain outside the project’s license grant. [Software license](./LICENSE)
